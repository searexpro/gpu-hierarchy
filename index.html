Data Sources

Primary: Tom's Hardware GPU Benchmark Hierarchy, updated June 24, 2026 — 48 GPUs tested natively on Ryzen 7 9800X3D, 11 raster games, geometric mean fps, RTX 5090 as baseline. Three resolution tiers: 4K Ultra, 1440p Ultra, 1080p Ultra.

Secondary: Tom's Hardware 2022–2024 hierarchy (Core i9-12900K testbed) — used to project performance for cards not covered in the 2026 suite.

Tertiary: Tom's Hardware 2020–2021 composite scores — used only for cards not covered in the 2022–2024 suite.

Projection Methodology

Era priority: 2026 native data > 2022–2024 measured data > 2020–2021 composite score.

Local 10-anchor projection: For each card requiring projection, the 10 nearest-performing anchor cards (by fps at each resolution tier) in the overlap between datasets are identified. Their cross-era conversion ratios are averaged to project the target card's performance. This outperforms both all-anchor and downward-only methods in internal validation (MAPE reduction of ~30% at 1440p and 4K vs. all-anchor).

Missing tiers: Cards without 4K or 1440p data have those tiers extrapolated from the same local-anchor method within the 2026 suite. Extrapolated values are shown in italic.

Laptop GPUs: Each laptop GPU is tied to one ratio against its same-name desktop card, sourced from Notebookcheck's own direct laptop-vs-desktop statements and measured multi-game comparisons, cross-checked against Tom's Hardware's desktop figures already in this dataset. One ratio is applied uniformly across 1080p/1440p/4K, since resolution-specific laptop data isn't reliably available from a single consistent source. The gap ranges from about 11% at the low end of a generation's lineup to as much as 85% at the flagship tier — desktop cards get cut down harder at the low end (narrower memory bus, fewer cores) while laptop cards are relatively more power-limited at the top, so the two curves aren't parallel. The desktop card always wins; where the gap is unusually small, that's a verified real finding, not an estimate that broke.

Console rasterization: Each console's figure is a blend of two real desktop GPUs, chosen from the best matched-hardware testing available for that device rather than compute-unit or teraflop specs alone (which routinely overstate console performance by a full tier). PS5 = 50% RX 6700 10GB + 50% RTX 2070 Super, per Digital Foundry's own on-record statement that PS5 "is still broadly equivalent to a 2070 Super, slightly worse than a 2080." Xbox Series X = 50% RX 6700 XT + 50% RTX 2080, per Digital Foundry's own early performance testing naming RTX 2080 directly — not the RX 6800 its CU count would imply. PS5 Pro = 50% RX 7700 XT + 50% RX 6800, chosen over RTX 3070 Ti (architecturally comparable, but its 8GB VRAM understates 4K performance in a way the console itself doesn't share). Steam Machine = 50% (90% of RX 7600) + 50% RX 6600 — not RX 7600 alone, despite the chip being an architecturally cut-down RX 7600 die (28 of 32 CUs); Gamers Nexus's retail-hardware review found real performance lands closer to RX 6600, with RX 7600 typically running ahead of it. Steam Deck = 50% GTX 1050 + 50% GTX 1050 Ti, and Xbox Series S = 50% GTX 1650 Super + 50% RX 5500 XT, both from consensus ranges across independent testing (Series S sourcing is the weakest of the six). Console 4K figures represent PC Ultra equivalent — actual console 4K uses dynamic resolution and upscaling.

Console ray tracing: Extended to all six devices using the same matched-testing-first principle as rasterization. PS5 = 50% RTX 2060 + 50% RX 6700, and Xbox Series X = 50% RTX 2060 Super + 50% RX 6700 — non-XT even on the Series X side, since RX 6700 XT's own RT figure would push the estimate above the ceiling Digital Foundry's testing establishes. PS5 Pro = RTX 3070 alone, per Digital Foundry's Alan Wake 2 analysis. Steam Machine = 90% of RX 7600 alone, not blended with RTX 3060 — RX 7600 measurably underperforms RTX 3060 in ray tracing specifically (up to 22% slower in third-party testing), which makes a blend internally inconsistent even though it works fine for rasterization. Xbox Series S has no tested RT equivalent; its figure is derived by applying its compute-unit ratio to Series X's own RT figure (38.5%) and is flagged as a derived estimate, not a tested one. Steam Deck also has no equivalent card — its GPU has real hardware RT capability that its raster-anchor cards (GTX 1050/1050 Ti) categorically lack — so its figure comes from Digital Foundry's own direct measurement (Metro Exodus Enhanced, ~30fps at 896×504), extrapolated to standard resolutions by assuming performance scales with pixel count. That extrapolation gets less reliable the further from the tested resolution: treat 1080p as a reasonable estimate, 1440p as weaker, and 4K as directional only. See the confidence markers below for exactly how many estimation steps sit behind any individual number.

Faster Than % of Steam Users: Sourced from Valve's July 2026 Steam Hardware & Software Survey. Steam names about 90 cards individually and folds everything else into a generic "Other" bucket (8.7% of respondents). Of those ~90 named cards, some are excluded from matching entirely — generic integrated-graphics labels, and untracked variants this tool doesn't carry (e.g. laptop RTX 3050). Where Steam merges two memory capacities of the same card into one undifferentiated line (RTX 4060 Ti, RTX 5060 Ti, RX 9060 XT), that share is assigned to the more powerful variant (16GB) rather than excluded — a deliberate conservative choice: it only changes the result for cards whose performance falls between the two variants, and the trade-off is that the slower (8GB) variant's own percentile doesn't get credit for any of that shared line. What's left is 57 cards matched by name to a card in this tool, covering 62.8% of the surveyed population — that 62.8%, not 100%, is the denominator. For any card, its percentage is the summed survey share of every matched card with a lower composite score (never counting itself or a tie), divided by 62.8%. This is why even the RTX 5090 doesn't show 100.0% — a small remainder of matched cards can still land above it in edge cases, and the figure is a share of the identifiable population, not a claim about literally every Steam user. Shown for every card in this tool, including laptops and consoles, since it only needs a composite score, not an actual Steam listing.

Confidence markers (*): Every calculated value carries a mark showing how many steps removed it is from an actual measurement — hover any marked value to see the specific chain. No mark = directly measured. * = one step (e.g. a 2022-2024-tested card projected to 2026). ** = two steps (e.g. the 2020-2021 → 2022-2024 → 2026 double-hop, or a laptop ratio built on an already-projected desktop reference). *** = three steps — typically a console figure built by blending two cards that are themselves projections. **** = four or more steps, or a derived estimate rather than a tested equivalent (e.g. Xbox Series S ray tracing, scaled from Series X's own already-blended figure; Steam Deck ray tracing at 1440p/4K, extrapolated across resolutions from one real on-device measurement at a much lower resolution). More marks isn't a defect — it's this tool telling you exactly how far a number sits from something anyone actually measured, so you can weigh it accordingly.

Limitations

All projected figures are estimates. Real-world performance varies by game, driver version, CPU, and memory configuration. The benchmark suite uses specific game titles whose characteristics may not represent all gaming workloads. Composite average weights all three resolution tiers equally.

This tool is intended for relative comparison only. Figures should not be interpreted as guaranteed performance.
